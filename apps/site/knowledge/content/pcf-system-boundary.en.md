# ISO 14067: how to set the system boundary for a product footprint

- Type: method guide
- Industry: textiles / packaging (useful for most manufacturers)
- Service: PCF
- Updated: 2026-10-30
- Readers: plants and supply-chain teams asked for product carbon footprints

## In one line

Before any maths, lock three things: **which product, how far the system goes, and the functional unit**. A fuzzy boundary forces rework on every dataset.

## Boundary in plain language

The system boundary is where the study **starts, stops, and which processes count**.

Two common options:

| Label | Meaning | When it shows up |
|---|---|---|
| Cradle-to-gate | Raw materials through factory gate | Very common in brand / procurement requests |
| Cradle-to-grave | Through use and end-of-life | More complete, heavier data and responsibility |

For many export manufacturers, **locking cradle-to-gate first** is enough. If the customer needs more, change the boundary in writing.

## Four items to freeze before modelling

1. **Product object** — which SKU / product family; do not mix
2. **Functional unit** — e.g. “1 tonne of product” or “1 finished piece”
3. **Life-cycle stages** — materials, transport, production, subcontracting… in or out
4. **Cut-off rules** — what tiny flows may be omitted, and the rule in writing

## Common plant pitfalls

- Functional unit agreed verbally, never written
- Subcontract dyeing / finishing included by one party, excluded by another
- Steam and electricity at shop totals that do not map to one product
- Customer asks “to gate”, team collects full life-cycle data unnecessarily

## Three moves

1. Confirm in writing with the customer: boundary type + functional unit + period
2. List in-boundary processes (including subcontractors)
3. Only then issue collection sheets — **boundary before numbers**
